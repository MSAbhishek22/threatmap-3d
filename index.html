The problem

Attacks are not single events.
They are webs.

One break-in is rarely one thing. A phishing trojan opens the door, a second tool takes control, a ransomware crew walks in last. The pieces have names — malware, domains, servers, file hashes — but a list of names does not show how they hold together.

ThreatMap 3D pins the pieces to a board and draws the string between them, in 3D, so you can walk around the web and read it the way an analyst does: what is this, what does it touch, and what breaks if we pull it out?

    Why a board, not a list

    A list tells you what exists. A board tells you what matters. On this board you can see in seconds that one delivery network fed half the ransomware era, that a single shared tool quietly joins two stories that look unrelated, and that some of the worst days on the internet turned on a $10 domain registration.

    That is the habit threat intelligence runs on: follow the string, ask what else it touches, and defend the connections — not just the names.

    Honest note on the data: every pin and string on this board is a teaching sample drawn from public reporting — vendor research, government advisories and court records, listed in DATA_SOURCE.md in the project folder. Nothing here is live, scraped or secret, and the years are "first seen in public reporting", which is how analysts date these things.
